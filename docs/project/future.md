# Future

## Short-Term — Platform Optimization {#short-term-platform-optimization}

![Short-term vision for a data-driven ORCA optimization platform](/assets/images/submission-20261017/future/short-term-platform-optimization.png)

*Figure 1. Proposed ORCA development loop connecting computational design, POD–CETUS optimization, SONAR measurement, future reporter-RNA calibration, and system-level integration.*

*From Engineering an Editor to Engineering Discovery*

Our work began with APOE4 RNA editing, but it has grown into a broader effort to engineer how RNA editors are designed, controlled, delivered, and measured. POD explored the PUF recognition architecture; CETUS investigated the APOBEC catalytic domain; Integrated ORCA brought the two into the same optimization space; and SONAR introduced a quantitative framework for evaluating editing behavior. Together with computational design and expression control, these efforts suggest a new possibility: **what if every editing experiment could inform the next design?** Our next step is not simply to improve one ORCA variant, but to make the process of developing RNA editors more systematic, predictive, and iterative.

That shift begins with **joint optimization of recognition and catalysis**. Through POD, we investigated how PUF recognition length, tripartite recognition motifs (TRMs), and repeat-level mutations shape RNA targeting. CETUS approached the same challenge from the catalytic side, exploring how APOBEC modifications affect editing activity and specificity. Integrated ORCA then exposed an important design consideration: a promising PUF variant and a promising APOBEC variant do not necessarily form the best pair. Their performance depends on the complete molecular context. We therefore aim to expand the combinatorial exploration of these two modules, using on-target editing, target-site selectivity, and transcriptome-wide bystander profiles to identify patterns of compatibility rather than optimizing each component in isolation.

**Computation could turn those patterns into more informed experiments.** PUFFER currently provides a workflow for evaluating potential PUF target sites; we envision extending that foundation with the experimental knowledge generated by POD, CETUS, and Integrated ORCA. Future candidate assessment could jointly consider target-sequence features, local RNA structure, editor architecture, and competing sites across the transcriptome. The goal is not merely to predict which variant might produce the highest editing percentage. It is to prioritize designs for a particular RNA target and application, balancing activity, specificity, and potential off-target risk. As matched datasets accumulate, such models could narrow the experimental search space and reveal relationships that are difficult to infer from any single screening campaign. Eventually, a new disease-associated target might no longer require starting the entire optimization process from scratch.

Better design, however, depends on **better feedback**. SONAR has established a layered approach to characterizing editing-associated fluorescence, but fluorescence is not yet a validated substitute for the actual fraction of edited reporter RNA. Establishing a reliable **Predicted Editing Rate (PER)** for reporter RNA is therefore a major next objective. We plan to collect matched flow-cytometry and reporter RNA-sequencing data across ORCA variants, expression doses, and cellular contexts, then evaluate calibration models using independent validation, prediction-error analysis, and clearly defined operating ranges. This would move SONAR beyond relative comparisons toward evidence-supported estimates of reporter RNA editing under conditions where the calibration has been demonstrated to hold. A subsequent challenge will be to investigate how reporter-based measurements relate to editing of endogenous APOE, rather than assuming that the two are interchangeable.

These advances could ultimately connect in a **data-driven design–build–test–learn loop**. PUFFER would help prioritize candidates; engineered ORCA variants would be built and tested; SONAR would provide rapid quantitative feedback; and RNA sequencing would anchor that feedback to molecular evidence. The resulting data could then refine the next round of computational design. In this vision, experimental results would cease to be only endpoints: they would become reusable knowledge about how RNA recognition, catalytic context, and editing outcomes interact. The ambition is not an autonomous discovery system already in hand, but a development workflow in which computational prediction and wet-lab evidence progressively improve one another.

At the same time, a useful RNA-editing platform must be judged by more than editing efficiency. We plan to extend system-level validation of AAV9 delivery, characterize gfaABC1D-driven expression across relevant cell types, and assess how ASO-based suppression and Cre–loxP-mediated shutdown could function within a complete ORCA system. These efforts would bring delivery compatibility, cellular selectivity, controllability, and bystander risk into the same evaluation framework as on-target performance. The next generation of ORCA should not simply be a stronger editor for APOE4. **It should be a platform that helps us design, measure, compare, and improve RNA editors for the targets that come next.**

## Long-Term — Therapeutic Translation {#long-term-therapeutic-translation}

![Long-term ORCA vision from astrocyte RNA editing toward therapeutic translation](/assets/images/submission-20261017/future/long-term-therapeutic-translation.png)

*Figure 2. Proposed translational path from improved CNS delivery and astrocytic APOE4 RNA editing to potential local tissue effects, functional studies, safety evaluation, and clinical strategy.*

*Beyond the Edited Cell*

If precise RNA editing changes the molecular state of one cell, could that change extend beyond the cell itself—and eventually influence the course of disease? This is the question that motivates our long-term vision for ORCA. Correcting APOE4 RNA to an APOE3-equivalent sequence is a molecular starting point, not a therapeutic endpoint. Alzheimer's disease involves interactions among astrocytes, neurons, other glial cells, and their shared environment. The greater challenge is to determine whether targeted molecular correction in a subset of cells can produce meaningful changes in the surrounding neural tissue. Moving from a successful C-to-U edit to a potential treatment will require us to connect molecular precision with biological function.

The first frontier is **delivery that serves the therapeutic strategy**. We initially chose AAV9 because its potential for sustained expression complements ORCA's relatively compact architecture. Published mouse studies provide a CNS-delivery precedent for AAV9 [3](#future-ref-3). Future capsid-engineered AAV systems could offer improved CNS delivery and more selective access to astrocytes, following preclinical capsid-engineering precedents [4](#future-ref-4). Nonviral alternatives, including lipid nanoparticles (LNPs) and extracellular vesicles (EVs), also have preclinical RNA-delivery precedents [5](#future-ref-5), [6](#future-ref-6). For ORCA, these remain routes to investigate for transient editor exposure, repeat dosing, or adjustable treatment regimens; compatibility with our cargo, cell targets and safety requirements would need independent evaluation. Because RNA editing does not permanently rewrite genomic DNA, continuous editor expression need not be the only path worth pursuing. Combined with further development of ASO regulation and Cre–loxP shutdown, these delivery options raise a more useful set of questions: **which cells should receive ORCA, when should editing begin, how long should it continue, and when should it stop?**

An even more compelling question concerns **how far the effect of an edited astrocyte might reach**. ApoE is not confined to the cell that produces it; astrocytes secrete ApoE in lipid-associated particles that participate in lipid transport and intercellular interactions [1](#future-ref-1), [2](#future-ref-2). This suggests a possibility worth testing: if only some astrocytes undergo APOE4-to-APOE3-equivalent RNA editing, could changes in the extracellular ApoE environment benefit neighboring cells that were never edited? Edited astrocytes might contribute ApoE3-like proteins to the local lipoprotein pool, potentially changing lipid handling and downstream cellular responses. In a human co-culture setting under specific lipid-stress conditions, APOE3 astrocytes have been reported to alleviate lipid accumulation and neurite abnormalities in APOE4 neurons (Halim et al., 2026; [1](#future-ref-1)). That observation provides biological motivation—not proof that ORCA will reproduce the effect. Our task is to determine whether RNA editing of astrocytic APOE can generate a comparable, non-cell-autonomous benefit.

This leads to a central translational question: **how much correction is enough?** We envision using human iPSC-derived astrocytes to generate cell populations with different degrees of editing coverage, followed by mixed cultures, astrocyte–neuron co-cultures, and eventually brain organoid models. Rather than examining the editing rate alone, we would connect three levels of measurement: the proportion of cells successfully reached and edited, the extent of APOE RNA editing within those cells, and the composition of secreted ApoE proteins. We could then test how these variables relate to lipid homeostasis, neuronal function, and amyloid-β-associated biology. The aim is to establish whether tissue-level responses depend on editing coverage, editing duration, spatial distribution, or some combination of these factors. If a measurable functional benefit emerges before every target cell is edited, that relationship could reshape the delivery and dosing goals for ORCA.

Only with such human-relevant evidence could ORCA advance toward **preclinical therapeutic evaluation**. Suitable APOE4 animal models would allow us to examine brain distribution, cell-type preference, editing persistence, and disease-related functional outcomes in vivo. Safety assessment would need to extend beyond confirming the intended edit: transcriptome-wide unintended editing, prolonged expression, immune responses, dose selection, and the reliability of shutdown mechanisms would all require systematic investigation. Further ahead, a clinical strategy would have to account for patient heterogeneity. APOE genotype, disease stage, and biomarkers from blood, cerebrospinal fluid, and neuroimaging could inform future participant stratification and outcome selection, drawing on established Alzheimer’s diagnostic and staging frameworks [7](#future-ref-7). Which populations might benefit from ORCA would still require dedicated evidence. For an intervention directed at an Alzheimer's-related risk mechanism, timing may be especially important: could the greatest benefit come before extensive neural damage, rather than only after symptoms are established?

Our long-term vision also reaches beyond APOE4. As programmable recognition, catalytic design, delivery, and safety control improve, the same principles could be explored for other disease-relevant RNAs amenable to C-to-U editing. The nonpermanent nature of RNA editing offers a distinct therapeutic design space, but its value will depend on whether molecular effects can become sufficiently durable, reproducible, and controllable to matter biologically. Ultimately, ORCA is about more than changing one nucleotide. **It is about learning whether a precisely targeted molecular intervention can improve the behavior of a complex biological system—and what evidence is needed to turn that possibility into a treatment.**
