## A question that stayed {#0-introduction-life-has-more-than-one-bandwidth}

“What can we do with this once we have extracted it?”

At Taoyuan Subdistrict Elderly Service Center, we helped older adults extract DNA from bananas. As the activity ended, one participant asked this question. We had prepared the science and the steps, but her question made us realize how little we had said about why any of it might matter in her daily life.

We kept returning to her question as we planned later activities. Before deciding what to teach or build, we needed to understand what people wanted to know and where they needed support.

ORCA studies RNA editing for Alzheimer's disease (AD). Building on a published PUF–APOBEC RNA-editing platform [2], we aim to edit APOE4 mRNA towards an APOE3-like protein product. The project is still at the research stage. Carrying APOE ε4 increases risk; it does not mean someone has AD.[1]

Clinicians helped us rethink delivery and treatment burdens. Older adults and care staff helped us revise a workbook and try a home-safety tool. Our courses, learning materials and outreach are documented on the [Education page](/education).

Our approach is **Emit, Echo, Evolve: ask, listen, change.** People join us with different needs and ways of learning. Their experience helps shape what we do next.

### What people want to know {#2-2-public-understanding-survey-addressing-what-people-want-to-know-first}

We collected **103 valid survey responses** to learn what people want to know about RNA editing. Of these, 96 respondents were aged 18–30; only five had direct experience of cognitive disorders or caregiving. These findings mainly reflect younger respondents.

Respondents most often prioritized practical improvement (68.9%), serious adverse effects (54.4%) and who the technology is for (46.6%).

A question about a hypothetical biomarker reduction also showed why we must distinguish changes in a measurement from benefits in daily life. Full wording and results are in the report.

Vague claims such as “effective” and too much jargon made explanations harder to follow. Illustrations and one-page summaries were among the preferred formats.

**We put four questions first: what could improve, what are the risks, who is it for, and how strong is the evidence?** Short summaries come before technical detail.

### Supporting informed choices {#1-2-informed-consent-and-proxy-decision-making-designing-multiple-prototype-versions}

**A signature does not tell us what someone understood.** We want research information to help people ask questions and make their own choices.

Our planned prototype starts with a short explanation of purpose, research stage, possible benefits, risks and alternatives. Readers could then choose more detail. Large-print, audio and assisted-reading versions still need development and testing.

We also plan prompts that invite readers to explain key points in their own words. These should help us improve the explanation, without making reading ability a barrier to participation.

Older age, cognitive impairment or family accompaniment should not automatically be treated as an inability to decide. Support and proxy decisions must follow appropriate ethical and professional processes; a communication tool cannot replace them.[3]

### Future use {#1-3-product-development-and-commercialization-translational-questions-and-future-interviews}

Future use would depend on delivery, manufacturing, clinical evidence, affordability and ongoing support. We need to understand who would use ORCA and who would carry those responsibilities.

We completed initial Builder interviews at hackathon booths about products addressing bereavement and memory, mothers’ and homemakers’ sense of self-worth, and sign-language learning. For ORCA, these conversations raised questions about respecting personal experiences of memory, care and communication. They did not establish clinical outcomes or changes to our RNA-editing system. The interviews and planned deeper follow-ups are documented on our [Inclusivity page](/inclusivity#builder-interviews).

### Changing direction {#1-4-adjusting-the-implementation-path-from-a-bone-conduction-hardware-concept-to-a-software-tool}

Our conversations with **Shokz** began with an idea for our own workplace. Could bone-conduction headphones carry an AI assistant into a synthetic biology laboratory? We imagined spoken reminders to take a break or guidance through experimental steps, leaving a researcher's hands free. It was an appealing starting point, but the laboratory's firm safety requirements left little room for the use we had imagined. We set that application aside rather than treating safety as a detail to resolve after designing the product.

#### Looking beyond the laboratory

We then explored a different setting: everyday activity among older adults. Many older people remain active, and we wondered whether a familiar wearable could offer support without asking them to give up that independence. Our working analogy was a phone watch designed for an older person: a way to stay connected and find help while going about their day.

We considered adding GPS so that, if a wearer appeared uncertain about their route, the device might offer directions home and reassuring audio. We also wondered whether selected audio stimulation could have a role in supporting cognitive health. Another idea was to use head-mounted motion sensing to detect a possible fall and connect it with an inflatable protective device worn around the waist or back. **These were exploratory concepts.** We had not validated a way to infer disorientation, established a cognitive benefit from audio, or demonstrated fall protection. Whether head-mounted sensing could improve on a watch was a question for testing, not a result we had obtained.

Behind these ideas was a concern we wanted to keep: how could someone continue to move through a familiar world with confidence, while having support available when they needed it?

#### Why the collaboration did not proceed

Further discussions brought the practical requirements into focus. Shokz explained that it did not have a dedicated team for this particular application, so development would likely need support from a university laboratory. From our conversations, its priorities were connecting with innovative talent and developing university–industry relationships; demand for a new product or product promotion was less central.

We approached university laboratories in the hope of finding that technical support. The discussions did not produce a shared starting point. The laboratories we approached were looking primarily for research funding or substantial hardware support, while the opportunity we could bring centred on talent and an exploratory collaboration. We had found interest on both sides, but had not found an arrangement that met both sides' needs.

**The proposed collaboration did not proceed.** There was no jointly developed wearable or validated hardware outcome. This was disappointing, especially after imagining the everyday situations in which it might help. It also showed us something our early enthusiasm had missed: an idea needs people who can take responsibility for developing it, resources they can actually commit, and a reason for each partner to continue.

#### What we carried forward

We learned to ask about those conditions earlier, alongside the question of whether a product sounded useful. For future partnerships, we would need to clarify each side's priorities, available support and responsibilities before building a proposal around them.

Our attention then turned towards **Anjia Guardian**, a mini-app for home-safety checks, reminders and care records. It offered a more manageable form for the team to build and revise with older adults. The wearable exploration had ended, but the concern behind it remained: support should help people retain a say in their everyday lives. We wanted our next step to begin with that concern and with a clearer understanding of what we could carry through.

### Anjia Guardian {#2-5-anjia-guardian-a-home-safety-mini-app-designed-for-older-adults}

Some needs could be addressed while ORCA's research continues. We developed **Anjia Guardian**, a home-safety mini-app, with a web version and code package.

It brings together photo-based home observations, safety self-checks, medication reminders, kitchen timers, an SOS dialling entry point and a care log.

Large text, larger buttons and confirmations support key actions. Older adults and family members can review prompts and records in their respective use contexts.

**Older adults in Shahe tried it and told us they found it useful.** This early feedback gave us a clearer setting for improving the interface and prompts; it does not establish safety outcomes.

## Revisiting our map {#stakeholder-map-review}

At the **Peking–Rice Global Summit on August 30, 2026**, we presented the first version of our stakeholder map.

Presenting our first stakeholder map made us pause. Its nine stages, from the laboratory to the community, felt natural as we talked through them. Yet that smooth sequence did not explain how we had identified the stakeholders or analysed their relationships with ORCA. We could tell a story about the chain more easily than we could explain the evidence behind its structure. The long layout also made those relationships difficult to follow.

This became a starting point for revising the map. We needed to ask why each group belonged, what we had actually learned about their concerns, how they could affect or be affected by ORCA, and which relationships still needed investigation. The original slide records our thinking at the Summit; its checkmarks should not be read as a current audit of completed stakeholder consultations. A revised map will need to make the reasoning and remaining gaps clearer before we assess whether it is easier to understand.

## Thank you {#acknowledgements}

We began this year with a research idea and many questions. We end it with people to thank. Behind the changes on this page are people who gave us their time, shared something from their lives, or asked a question we had not thought to ask. We want to remember what each of you helped us see.

**To the older adults, families and care staff who welcomed us:** thank you to everyone at **Shahe and Taoyuan Subdistrict Elderly Service Centers**, and especially to **Ms. Wu (a pseudonym)**. You helped us notice the small things that decide whether something is useful: print that is easy to read, room to write a question, instructions that fit into an ordinary day. Thank you for trying our activities, workbook and Anjia Guardian, and for telling us where they needed work. To the participant who asked what the extracted DNA could do for her: your question stayed with us long after the activity ended. It still reminds us to ask what our work offers the person sitting across from us.

**To the experts who helped us think more carefully:** thank you to **Dr. Zhifan Zhou, Professor Keqiang Ye and Dr. Ziwei Dai**. You brought delivery, safety, treatment burdens and the freedom to choose into our discussions. You helped us see the family, the appointment and the repeated dose behind a proposed treatment. We are grateful for your time and for the difficult questions that gave our next steps more purpose.

**To the builders who shared their products with us:** thank you for helping us think about the people behind memory, care and communication. We carry those questions into our reflection on ORCA. Our fuller account of these conversations is on the [Inclusivity page](/inclusivity#builder-interviews).

**To our own team, instructors and advisors:** thank you for the work behind every visit, lesson, model, tool and page. Much of that work is easy to miss in a finished photograph. We want to thank the people who prepared materials, arranged interviews, wrote and revised explanations, built the tools, and helped the rest of us carry an idea through to something we could share. This page belongs to your effort as much as to the people pictured on it.

Some people appear here by name; others are part of a classroom, a care center, a team or a conversation. We are grateful to all of you. We cannot yet promise what ORCA will become. We can keep listening, be honest about what remains unfinished, and let what you have taught us change what we do next. Thank you for trusting us with a little of your time and experience. We hope to take good care of it.
