## Learning goals and audiences {#learning-goals-and-audiences}

We work with secondary-school learners, older adults, younger children and peers from different fields. Our aims are to help them connect biology with familiar questions, examine evidence, ask what a design assumes and explain a possible next step.

A classroom needs tasks that fit prior study and available time. A workbook must also work when a reader returns to it at home. Younger children need things to observe and handle, while a design challenge gives peers a reason to question one another’s choices. We adapted the teaching format to these settings.

## Bringing synthetic biology into the classroom {#classroom-synthetic-biology}

### Comparing starting points {#classroom-comparison}

With **CUHK-HongKong-SBS** and **HG-Tokyo**, we compared curriculum and assessment documents to decide where an introductory synthetic biology course could connect to existing lessons. A common starting point matters: a task that assumes prior biotechnology study may need a different introduction in another classroom.

We studied four curriculum configurations: Pearson Edexcel iAL, Hong Kong HKDSE, mainland Chinese biology and the gaokao, and a study-specific combination of Japanese high school biology with AP Biology.

CUHK-HongKong-SBS contributed the iAL and HKDSE analyses, SUSTech-BIO the mainland analysis, and HG-Tokyo the combined Japan + AP analysis. We used a provisional reference framework covering **molecular foundations; biotechnology tools; synthetic biology concepts; design–build–test–learn; data, modelling and research literacy; and bioethics, biosafety and society**.

For each domain, we recorded curriculum and examination coverage separately on a 0–3 scale: absent, basic, intermediate and advanced. Each rating is linked to supporting syllabus, textbook and examination material. Score ranges and elective qualifications preserve differences within the configurations. Japan + AP represents the combined programme reviewed by HG-Tokyo.

One useful contrast appears in report pages 2 and 5: **all four configurations receive 3 for molecular foundations, while design–build–test–learn receives 1–2 for iAL, HKDSE and mainland biology, and 2 for Japan + AP**, in both curriculum and examination columns. The report describes experimental reasoning and suggestions for improvement, but less explicit experience of a whole biological engineering cycle.

The teaching implication is to connect familiar gene expression or protein structure to a purposeful modification, then ask how to test and revise it. The ratings describe the depth of coverage in the reviewed documents.

The resulting design direction emphasizes familiar problems, explicit design choices and revision. Our **Education Transfer** website makes the four curriculum configurations available for interactive comparison. Drag one configuration onto another, or use the click and keyboard controls, to explore curriculum and assessment coverage across the six domains, choose a focus, and download a source-linked report.

### From documents to classroom constraints {#classroom-teacher-advice}

We also asked two Yali High School biology teachers what would work within a real lesson.

**Jiaju Li**, Head of Biology Teaching and Research, described strong training in scientific reasoning but limited time for hands-on work. He suggested starting with genetic and protein engineering.

His example was improving natural insulin: identify a problem, propose a change, decide how to test it, then revise. Short cases could fit regular lessons; longer projects could suit clubs.

**Sijie Wang** suggested a 12–15-lesson elective using cases, worksheets and simulated experiments. Fermentation, microbes or environmental topics could offer familiar starting points.

The interviews also brought the practical conditions of a lesson into focus: Jiaju Li distinguished knowing PCR or electrophoresis procedures from having performed them, while Sijie Wang located a possible elective in Grade 11, after students have acquired some relevant knowledge. Time and examination pressure influenced the feasible format as much as equipment did.

### Designing classroom tasks {#classroom-design-progress}

The following proposed task sequence brings together the curriculum comparison and the teachers’ advice: start with familiar biology, make a design choice, then explain how to test and revise it.

| Evidence informing the design | Learning task | What a future class could leave for review |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Molecular foundations are already prominent; explicit engineering iteration is less so. | Start with Li’s insulin example: identify a limitation, connect structure to function and propose one modification. | An annotated design with an explanation of the intended change. |
| Teachers describe limited practical time and a gap between knowing procedures and using them. | In a short lesson, work with a simplified case or simulated result; reserve extended investigations for an elective or club. | A test plan naming controls, expected observations and a result that would challenge the idea. |
| Both teachers want inquiry beyond standard examination responses. | Ask learners to revise their proposal after considering evidence, risks and unanswered questions. | A second version explaining what changed and why. |

The provisional framework, comparison report, Education Transfer tool and two teacher interviews are complete. The proposed classroom tasks are at the design stage, with short lessons and a 12–15-period elective as possible formats. Our [exchanges with high-school teams](#peer-learning) offered a separate setting for presenting and discussing research ideas.

When Ms. Wu reviewed the workbook, she pointed out that the small type and dense pages would make it difficult for some older adults to read. Some liked to write down what they learned, so she suggested leaving space for notes as well as adding pictures and reducing text. Alongside participants' feedback, her advice helped us see the workbook as something people would use in their own way, and she reminded us to consider printing costs too.

We revised the workbook around those needs: shorter reading sections, illustrations, writing space and topics readers could choose for themselves.

**For older learners:** each chapter has two pages, with pictures, short steps, a shared practice activity and three things to remember. We simplified long sentences and teaching instructions so readers can find a useful action more easily. Practical safety reminders remain beside the relevant activity.

**For use at home:** the student appendices include a medicine card, an emergency contact card, four weekly pages for morning and evening blood pressure records, and another four for blood sugar records. There are also a four-week action plan and four Cornell note pages, with separate areas for key words and questions, main notes and an after-class summary. Measuring schedules follow each reader’s healthcare professional’s advice.

**For teachers:** the guide retains the fuller teaching material and adds a suggested lesson sequence, demonstrations and assisted ways to participate. Teachers and learners can follow the same chapter names. Personal record sheets and note pages are kept in the student books, so the teacher’s guide does not duplicate them.

**Pictures and language:** we replaced unclear illustrations with simpler AI-assisted drawings and added all diagram labels separately using real fonts, with consistent lettering within each language edition. The English student edition also adapts the illustrated people for an international audience, uses local emergency-number prompts and identifies services that are specific to China.

### Physical activities and software development {#model-development-status}

The physical models support visual and tactile exploration through demonstration, close observation, handling and questions. Facilitators can prepare age-appropriate structure names and prompts, then use an observation sheet to keep track of the questions learners ask.

**TacTune is a software prototype** that extends model exploration through digital rotation, selection and sound. A calibrated camera interface is intended to connect pointing on a physical model with regional audio. This development sits alongside the physical teaching activities. Our [Inclusivity page](/inclusivity) explores how different experiences can inform participation and product design.

## WORLDPROOF: learning through critique and revision {#worldproof}

### Learning to question a design {#worldproof-process}

<div class="hp-media-row" markdown="1">

<div class="hp-media-copy" markdown="1">

We organized **WORLDPROOF**, a synthetic biology hackathon, to give people from different fields a chance to question a design together. We wanted teams to think about the people who would use it, the work of maintaining it and what might happen when things went wrong.

The theme was **“Build it. Break it. Fix it.”** The task asked participants from different fields to make a prototype, challenge assumptions and explain a revision. We wanted them to practise turning a question about real use into a change in a design.

</div>

| Stage | Participant task | Intended output |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Build | Choose a scenario, identify stakeholders and make a demonstrable proposal. | A first design and its assumptions. |
| Break | Challenge it with a failure, conflicting evidence or a stakeholder’s concern. Discussion topics included cost, use, maintenance, communication and trust. | The question raised and the assumption it challenges. |
| Fix | Change the design or its conditions of use, and explain the reason. | A revised proposal and a record of what still needs testing. |

### A task from the challenge pack {#worldproof-challenge-task}

**Riverlight**, the first of eight hypothetical scenarios, asks learners to consider contained living pollution sensors at urban wastewater outlets. Its stakeholders include residents downstream, operators, authorities, fishing communities and ecologists. Possible outputs include a sensor-status map, graded alerts or sample chain-of-custody records.

The pack’s “reality checks” include devices going offline, a signal that cannot be reproduced and a claim that the sensors caused pollution. These prompts give a facilitator concrete ways to question an apparently working design: what can an alert actually establish, who checks it and what happens when the system fails?

#### Biology with Qinglan Primary School {#qinglan-primary-online-biology-january-2026}

On **January 21, 2026**, we connected by video with children at **Qinglan Primary School in Haojiang District, Shantou, Guangdong**. Team members introduced everyday laboratory rules and explained Gram staining, showing how it distinguishes Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria. A live experiment helped the children follow the process as it happened.

At the end of the lesson, children raised their hands to ask questions. We left time for that curiosity, so the session could be a conversation as well as a demonstration.

## Evaluation and reusable resources {#evaluation-and-resources}

<span id="education-records"></span>

### Learning from questions and feedback {#education-evaluation}

The most useful feedback helped us see what made a learning activity relevant and manageable. At Taoyuan, a learner’s question about the use of extracted DNA reminded us to connect a demonstration with everyday concerns. The Yali teachers brought another practical question into focus: how could students move from familiar biology to design and revision within the time available for a lesson? Their suggestions helped us shape the proposed classroom tasks around cases, worksheets and simulations.

At Shahe, feedback concerned both the content and the way people wanted to use it. Ms. Wu’s comments about small print, dense text and space for notes helped us rethink the workbook as something to follow in class and return to at home. The revised editions make those choices visible through short steps, illustrations, shared practice and separate recording pages. The version comparison also distinguishes these responses from topics and formats added by the team.

The kindergarten teachers’ encouragement and Zhang’s account of his first hackathon offered perspectives on the experience of taking part. Alongside the activity photographs and materials, they help us reflect on observation, questions and discussion as ways into biology. We bring these sources together here as a retrospective account of teaching design and revision; the available records do not provide a measured estimate of learning gains.

Our next step for the classroom work is to develop the proposed sequence into lessons and try it with a teacher-selected group. We would look at how learners explain a modification, choose controls and revise an idea, while asking teachers about timing and difficulty. For the workbook, a further classroom trial would focus on finding a useful page, following its steps and writing comfortably in the printed copy. Print size, illustrations and production costs are part of that practical review.

For a future WORLDPROOF session, keeping first proposals, critique questions and revised versions would let us follow how a question changes a design. We also want to make the model activities easier for another facilitator to repeat, with age-appropriate prompts and notes on learners’ responses. TacTune remains a software prototype: testing with learners and facilitators, including blind learners, would guide work on camera reliability, independent use and understandable audio.

These follow-ups would connect the feedback we already use with learners’ explanations, work and use of the materials. Participation, enjoyment and self-reported change would be reported alongside assessed task performance, with each answering a different question about the activity. The [public RNA-editing survey](/human-practices#2-2-public-understanding-survey-addressing-what-people-want-to-know-first) continues to inform how we explain benefits, risks and evidence.

### Further development {#education-further-development}

#### A consent game · concept {#2-4-an-informed-consent-puzzle-game-practising-understanding-and-questioning-through-scenarios}

We proposed an **informed-consent puzzle game** where participants would find study information, spot what is missing and explain their questions.

The planned scenarios cover purpose, research stage, risks and alternatives. Text, illustrations and audio are formats we may compare.

**The game is still a concept.** Rules, prototypes and testing remain to be developed. We want to support questioning, rather than reward agreement.
