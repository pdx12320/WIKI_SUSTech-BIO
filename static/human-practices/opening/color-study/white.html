Ask. Listen. Change.

ORCA: On-target RNA Correction of APOE4 for Alzheimer's Disease

SUSTech-BIO
Emit, Echo, Evolve

Ask. Listen. Change.

People’s questions shape what we do next.

A question that stayed

“What can we do with this once we have extracted it?”

At Taoyuan Subdistrict Elderly Service Center, we helped older adults extract DNA from bananas. As the activity ended, one participant asked this question. We had prepared the science and the steps, but her question made us realize how little we had said about why any of it might matter in her daily life.

We kept returning to her question as we planned later activities. Before deciding what to teach or build, we needed to understand what people wanted to know and where they needed support.

ORCA studies RNA editing for Alzheimer's disease (AD). We aim to edit APOE4 mRNA towards an APOE3-like protein product.[2] The project is still at the research stage. Carrying APOE ε4 increases risk; it does not mean someone has AD.[1]

Clinicians helped us rethink delivery and treatment burdens. Older adults and care staff helped us revise a workbook and try a home-safety tool. Teachers helped us find better starting points for learning.

Our approach is Emit, Echo, Evolve: ask, listen, change. People join us with different needs and ways of learning. Their experience helps shape what we do next.

Shaping ORCA

We asked clinicians, researchers and care staff what our plans were missing. Their answers changed both our research priorities and how we explain ORCA.

People we listened to

Dr. Zhou: treatment burdens

Dr. Zhifan Zhou, Deputy Chief Physician in Neurology at Shenzhen People's Hospital, helped us look beyond the edit to the people who might one day receive it.

She described the care needed to assess memory problems and the growing role of blood biomarkers.[4] In her experience, diagnosis often follows clearer symptoms, though more people now seek help for mild memory decline.

Dr. Zhifan ZhouView their perspective ···
Dr. Zhifan Zhou

Interview insight · paraphrased

Her main question for ORCA was delivery: how could an editing system reach the brain? She also asked us to consider repeated doses, invasive procedures and how long an effect might last.

We gave delivery research greater priority and added treatment and caregiving burdens to our planning. A future treatment would ask patients and families to give time, money and effort. Those costs belong in the design discussion.

Prof. Ye: evidence and safety

Professor Keqiang Ye, Dean and Chair Professor at Shenzhen University of Advanced Technology, helped us ask what evidence an edit would need.

He advised us to study intervention timing, brain delivery and specificity together. Reaching the target site is only part of the question: nearby edits, changes elsewhere and differences between cell types also matter.

Prof. Keqiang YeView their perspective ···
Prof. Keqiang Ye

Interview insight · paraphrased

He reminded us that APOE has normal roles in the body. Editing rates alone cannot show whether those roles are preserved or whether disease-related changes improve.

His advice informed our assessments of editing efficiency and off-target effects, cell-type comparisons and delivery work. Methods and results are presented on the research pages.

Dr. Dai: room to choose

Dr. Ziwei Dai, Assistant Professor of Systems Biology at SUSTech, helped us think about the choices people face when a technology is still developing.

Dr. Ziwei DaiView their perspective ···
Dr. Ziwei Dai

Interview insight · paraphrased

Understanding a technology does not mean wanting to use it. Invasive delivery, costs, risks or limited evidence may be good reasons to wait.

She also raised APOE's roles outside the brain and the links between dose, cell state and unintended editing. These questions informed our continued evaluation.

We gave risks, costs, alternatives and uncertainty more space in our communication. Readers should have room to ask questions, wait or decline.

Ms. Wu: everyday care

Ms. Wu (a pseudonym), an administrative receptionist at Shahe Subdistrict Elderly Service Center, helped us understand everyday needs at the center.

Some older adults find small print, spoken explanations or dense text difficult to follow. Staff also have limited time. An activity needs to fit both the people attending and the people supporting it.

Her feedback guided our attention to readable materials and practical support. Later, it helped us revise the health workbook.

Supporting informed choices

A signature does not tell us what someone understood. We want research information to help people ask questions and make their own choices.

Our planned prototype starts with a short explanation of purpose, research stage, possible benefits, risks and alternatives. Readers could then choose more detail. Large-print, audio and assisted-reading versions still need development and testing.

We also plan prompts that invite readers to explain key points in their own words. These should help us improve the explanation, without making reading ability a barrier to participation.

Older age, cognitive impairment or family accompaniment should not automatically be treated as an inability to decide. Support and proxy decisions must follow appropriate ethical and professional processes; a communication tool cannot replace them.[3]

This remains a design proposal. We have not established its effectiveness. The goal is informed choice, including a choice researchers did not expect.

Future use

Future use would depend on delivery, manufacturing, clinical evidence, affordability and ongoing support. We need to understand who would use ORCA and who would carry those responsibilities.

We plan further conversations with RNA and AI entrepreneurs, screening-product developers, care professionals and home-adaptation providers. Interview records and resulting design decisions still need documentation.

Workbooks and model files can support others' work. Patent options for the editing technology need separate evaluation. We do not yet have evidence of patent filing or completed commercialization.

Changing direction

We considered collaborating with Shokz on bone-conduction reminders for older adults' daily safety.

Funding and technical constraints prevented a working hardware outcome. We changed direction towards something the team could build and revise with users.

That became Anjia Guardian, a mini-app for home-safety checks, reminders and care records. Older adults could try it and tell us what needed work.

Learning together

A familiar question, a page with room for notes, a model someone can hold: we tried different ways to make science easier to enter.

Starting in the classroom

With CUHK-HongKong-SBS and HG-Tokyo, we compared what students already learn before asking them to explore synthetic biology.

We studied four curriculum configurations: Pearson Edexcel iAL, Hong Kong HKDSE, mainland Chinese biology and the gaokao, and a study-specific combination of Japanese high school biology with AP Biology.

Our shared framework covered six areas, from molecular biology and biotechnology to design, research skills and ethics. Full methods are in the attached report.

The documents showed strong foundations in biology and experimental reasoning, with less explicit coverage of engineering design and complete design–build–test–learn cycles. This compares documents, not students' abilities.

We used the findings to focus on familiar problems, design choices and revision. We also created a web simulation to explore how exam questions connect with different curricula; its design will be documented separately.

Advice from teachers

We also asked two Yali High School biology teachers what would work within a real lesson.

Jiaju Li, Head of Biology Teaching and Research, described strong training in scientific reasoning but limited time for hands-on work. He suggested starting with genetic and protein engineering.

His example was improving natural insulin: identify a problem, propose a change, decide how to test it, then revise. Short cases could fit regular lessons; longer projects could suit clubs.

Sijie Wang suggested a 12–15-lesson elective using cases, worksheets and simulated experiments. Fermentation, microbes or environmental topics could offer familiar starting points.

We chose a clear starting point: build on what students know, then give them a problem they can design and rethink.

What people want to know

We collected 103 valid survey responses to learn what people want to know about RNA editing. Of these, 96 respondents were aged 18–30; only five had direct experience of cognitive disorders or caregiving. These findings mainly reflect younger respondents.

Respondents most often prioritized practical improvement (68.9%), serious adverse effects (54.4%) and who the technology is for (46.6%).

A question about a hypothetical biomarker reduction also showed why we must distinguish changes in a measurement from benefits in daily life. Full wording and results are in the report.

Vague claims such as “effective” and too much jargon made explanations harder to follow. Illustrations and one-page summaries were among the preferred formats.

We put four questions first: what could improve, what are the risks, who is it for, and how strong is the evidence? Short summaries come before technical detail.

A handbook shaped by readers

We wrote the Practical Healthy Living Workbook for Older Adults around everyday questions: medication, meals, movement, memory and medical visits.

The first edition gathered the information into chapters, summaries and self-check questions. Bringing it to Shahe showed us what still needed to change.

First edition

First workbook · Chinese PDF

At Shahe Subdistrict Elderly Service Center, we trialled lessons and discussed misleading health claims and scams. We focused on what to check and whom to ask before trusting a promise or making a purchase.

Older adults helped us see how the workbook would actually be used: read during a lesson, opened again at home, or written in while listening.

When Ms. Wu reviewed the workbook, she pointed out that the small type and dense pages would make it difficult for some older adults to read. Some liked to write down what they learned, so she suggested leaving space for notes as well as adding pictures and reducing text. Alongside participants' feedback, her advice helped us see the workbook as something people would use in their own way, and she reminded us to consider printing costs too.

We revised the workbook around those needs: shorter reading sections, illustrations, writing space and topics readers could choose for themselves.

It combines short health lessons with space to record bodily changes, medication questions and medical visits. Readers can also explore interests such as gardening, photography, crafts and music.

As we revised the handbook, older adults' reading habits and questions became part of its design. We wanted readers to be able to find a useful page, write down a concern or begin with something they enjoyed. Their experience helped us decide what deserved space.

A consent game · planned

We proposed an informed-consent puzzle game where participants would find study information, spot what is missing and explain their questions.

The planned scenarios cover purpose, research stage, risks and alternatives. Text, illustrations and audio are formats we may compare.

The game is still a concept. Rules, prototypes and testing remain to be developed. We want to support questioning, rather than reward agreement.

Anjia Guardian

Some needs could be addressed while ORCA's research continues. We developed Anjia Guardian, a home-safety mini-app, with a web version and code package.

It brings together photo-based home observations, safety self-checks, medication reminders, kitchen timers, an SOS dialling entry point and a care log.

Large text, larger buttons and confirmations support key actions. Older adults and family members can review prompts and records in their respective use contexts.

Older adults in Shahe tried it and told us they found it useful. This early feedback gave us a clearer setting for improving the interface and prompts; it does not establish safety outcomes.

WORLDPROOF

We organized WORLDPROOF, a synthetic biology hackathon, to give people from different fields a chance to question a design together. We wanted teams to think about the people who would use it, the work of maintaining it and what might happen when things went wrong.

The theme was “Build it. Break it. Fix it.” Teams made prototypes, challenged one another's assumptions, revised their designs and recorded the changes.

Everyday conversations

We shared science in care centers, on campus, at a kindergarten and through WeChat and Xiaohongshu. Each setting called for a different starting point.

Taoyuan: everyday questions

At Taoyuan, we paired brain-health and fraud-awareness discussions with banana DNA extraction. We talked about questioning exaggerated promises in health-product marketing.

This was where a participant asked what the extracted DNA could do for her. We carried that question into later activities: explain the connection to daily life, and leave time for what participants want to ask.

Campus: memory and time

With SUSTech-MED, we ran a campus charity-fair booth with activities including a “Time Mailbox.” Memory and time offered a familiar way to begin conversations about AD and ORCA.

Kindergarten: look and touch

At SUSTech Kindergarten, children explored our brain and cell models and observed simple yeast experiments. They could touch, look closely and ask about what they saw.

Thank you

We began this year with a research idea and many questions. We end it with people to thank. Behind the changes on this page are people who gave us their time, shared something from their lives, or asked a question we had not thought to ask. We want to remember what each of you helped us see.

To the older adults, families and care staff who welcomed us: thank you to everyone at Shahe and Taoyuan Subdistrict Elderly Service Centers, and especially to Ms. Wu (a pseudonym). You helped us notice the small things that decide whether something is useful: print that is easy to read, room to write a question, instructions that fit into an ordinary day. Thank you for trying our activities, workbook and Anjia Guardian, and for telling us where they needed work. To the participant who asked what the extracted DNA could do for her: your question stayed with us long after the activity ended. It still reminds us to ask what our work offers the person sitting across from us.

To the experts who helped us think more carefully: thank you to Dr. Zhifan Zhou, Professor Keqiang Ye and Dr. Ziwei Dai. You brought delivery, safety, treatment burdens and the freedom to choose into our discussions. You helped us see the family, the appointment and the repeated dose behind a proposed treatment. We are grateful for your time and for the difficult questions that gave our next steps more purpose.

To the entrepreneurs and industry professionals who joined our Builder interviews: thank you for making time for a student team and sharing your experience with us. We are still learning what it takes to carry a research idea into people's lives, and we are grateful that you let us bring our questions to you. As we continue to think about ORCA's future, we want to give the work beyond the laboratory the care and attention it deserves.

To the teachers and learners who shared their classrooms with us: thank you to Jiaju Li and Sijie Wang at Yali High School, the teachers and children of SUSTech Kindergarten, the teachers and students of the High School Affiliated to SUSTech, and everyone who joined our surveys, lessons and campus activities. You helped us find better ways to begin a conversation. Thank you to the SUSTech 3D Printing Club for turning our brain and cell designs into models children could hold. Seeing an idea become something another person can explore is a gift we will remember.

To the teams who made room for us: thank you to CUHK-HongKong-SBS and HG-Tokyo for the curriculum comparison; to NAU-China for bringing us into ICII and its Garden of Unity; and to SUSTech-MED and the University of Hong Kong iGEM team for the outreach and Grand APiC work we shared. Thank you to Cataverse-GreatBay, the HKUST (Guangzhou) community and iGBA representatives, and the teams we met throughout the year. Your willingness to share ideas made it easier for us to share our own unfinished work and learn from one another.

To everyone who built, questioned and revised with us at WORLDPROOF: thank you to Elżbieta Gralińska, Yannan Tian, Jacky Yuen, Duncan Chiu and Hengwei Su, and to every participant and volunteer. Your questions about cost, maintenance and trust helped bring our designs closer to everyday life. Thank you also to Zhang, the SUSTech bioinformatics student from the 2023 cohort, for sharing what your first hackathon meant to you. We wanted to make a place where people could try something together; your feedback gave us a reason to keep making that space.

To the people who brought our wider community together: thank you to the organizers, hosts, judges and volunteers of the Guangzhou and South China meetups, Tsinghua creative design competition, Grand APiC, SynBio Challenges and Peking–Rice Global Summit. We are grateful to the communities at South China Agricultural University, Shenzhen University, Tsinghua University and Shenzhen University of Advanced Technology, and to everyone who visited our booth, listened to a presentation or stayed to ask another question. Those conversations are part of this work too.

To our own team, instructors and advisors: thank you for the work behind every visit, lesson, model, tool and page. Much of that work is easy to miss in a finished photograph. We want to thank the people who prepared materials, arranged interviews, wrote and revised explanations, built the tools, and helped the rest of us carry an idea through to something we could share. This page belongs to your effort as much as to the people pictured on it.

Some people appear here by name; others are part of a classroom, a care center, a team or a conversation. We are grateful to all of you. We cannot yet promise what ORCA will become. We can keep listening, be honest about what remains unfinished, and let what you have taught us change what we do next. Thank you for trusting us with a little of your time and experience. We hope to take good care of it.

We are SUSTech-BIO, an interdisciplinary student team at Southern University of Science and Technology in Shenzhen. Through ORCA, we explore programmable RNA editing for Alzheimer’s disease, bringing together wet-lab engineering, computational modeling, and conversations with the people our work hopes to serve. We connect research with education, inclusive design, and public dialogue to build synthetic biology with care.
