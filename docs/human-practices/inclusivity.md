## Our Perspective: A Changing Spectrum {#our-perspective}

<span id="inclusion-across-a-spectrum"></span><span id="barriers-we-encountered"></span>

Last year, our team emphasized **two-way inclusion**. This year, our captain, Chenfan Liao, questioned whether it still implied two fixed sides: people offering an activity and people being included. We are rethinking our own practice through **spectrum-based inclusion**. Life holds many bands of experience; each person has their own, and it can change.

From childhood through youth and older age, health and living circumstances can change what someone needs. Others meet exclusion from the outset. **Spectrum** and **passband** are metaphors, not measurements or ranks. A passband is the range a setting routinely accommodates—perhaps written instructions, quick operation or specialist language. We ask how the environment can offer more entrances, while recognizing illness as part of life.

The three parts below explore changes across life stages, needs in memory, expression and perception, and the different experiences brought into WORLDPROOF. These perspectives overlap; they organize our activities without fixing people into groups or implying that we tracked each participant over a lifetime.

## 1. Across Life Stages {#across-life-stages}

### 1.1 Entering Science {#entering-science}

Entering a scientific discussion depends partly on what a lesson assumes. With **CUHK-HongKong-SBS** and **HG-Tokyo**, we compared four curriculum configurations to see where synthetic biology could connect to existing study. Familiar molecular foundations offered a starting point; a complete design–test–revise task often needed a more explicit introduction. The comparison describes curriculum and assessment documents, rather than measuring students’ abilities.

Two **Yali High School biology teachers** brought time and resources into the picture. **Jiaju Li** distinguished knowing PCR or electrophoresis procedures from having performed them, and suggested short genetic- or protein-engineering cases for regular lessons. **Sijie Wang** discussed a 12–15-lesson elective after students had acquired relevant prior knowledge. Their advice gave us ways to vary the length and practical demands of a task: a short simulated case, an extended elective or a club investigation.

The resulting classroom sequence remains a teaching design. Its access question is already concrete: how can someone make and question a biological design when their previous study, lesson time and equipment differ? The completed comparison, interviews and Education Transfer tool provide starting materials for adapting that entry point.

### 1.2 A Workbook Shaped by Older Readers {#workbook-access}

<span id="what-participants-changed"></span>

At **Shahe Subdistrict Elderly Service Center**, our first **Practical Healthy Living Workbook for Older Adults** was used in a facilitated lesson. Older readers and caregivers both participated in the workbook work. Their perspectives brought attention to following a lesson, returning to information at home and keeping useful notes.

The specific layout suggestions in the written account include those of **Ms. Wu (a pseudonym)**, a service-center staff member: small type and dense text made pages difficult to follow; pictures, fewer words and writing space could help. She also raised printing costs. We shortened student reading sections, added illustrations and steps, and placed note-taking and personal-record pages in the student book. A separate teacher’s guide holds the fuller teaching material.

### 1.3 Age-Friendly Digital Access {#age-friendly-digital-access}

<span id="anjia-task-access"></span>

**安家守护 (Anjia Guardian)** is the age-friendly Mini Program in this work. **Home Protection** is the name of the website built to demonstrate its workflow. The question is practical: can an older adult find a home-check task, understand the information it returns and involve a family member without navigating a dense interface?

Our documented delivery work brings together the supplied native Mini Program source and a separately built website walkthrough. The interface gives older adults and family members separate entrances. In the website’s older-adult view, “room check” leads to an illustrated room and a sample report. Numbered locations are paired with an explanation and a suggested action. The photograph selector says **local preview only**, making it possible to explore the example without taking or uploading a photograph. A confirmation step makes the demonstration SOS action explicit.

The [HP record](/human-practices#2-5-anjia-guardian-a-home-safety-mini-app-designed-for-older-adults) describes large text, larger controls, role-based navigation and confirmations in the Mini Program, and reports an early Shahe try-out with positive reactions. The exact version, assistance and feedback-led edits still need to be matched to that use record. The native source has a configurable cloud-recognition path; the website uses fixed examples and makes no AI assessment, call, notification or cross-device connection. These different forms give us separate tasks to check with users and developers.

## 2. When Needs Change {#when-needs-change}

### 2.1 Time Capsule {#time-capsule}

<span id="community-entry-points"></span>

**Time Capsule (时光信箱)** begins with one question:

<p class="inc-prompt">“If I lost my memory, what one sentence would I leave for myself?”</p>

The activity invites a person to consider what they would still want to preserve or communicate if their memory changed. Paper, writing materials and a mailbox turn that question into an action: leave a sentence for oneself. The content of the message belongs to the writer.

At a public-facing activity, this creates an entry into a difficult subject through a personal question. Someone can begin with what they want to keep or say, without first learning the vocabulary of AD or RNA editing. The activity’s materials and handwritten cards are documented in the design record; we present the invitation here without interpreting or reproducing individual responses.

[Activity materials and visual design](/design-communication#time-capsule)

### 2.2 Aphasia Map {#aphasia-map}

**Aphasia Map (失语地图)** gives two people a communication task with a changed rule. One draws an object card, avoids its name and the specified words, and uses gestures or another description to help a partner identify it. The instruction board makes the sequence visible in three steps.

<figure class="inc-instruction" id="aphasia-instructions">

The rule asks the speaker to find another way to convey an idea, and the listener to attend to those cues and check what they mean. This gives the pair a reason to discuss how communication depends on both people and the available channels. It is a communication game, not a clinical simulation of aphasia. The rules support that discussion; detailed accounts of how particular pairs responded are still being assembled.

### 2.3 Science through Touch and Sound {#tactune}

<span id="one-concept-different-ways-in"></span><span id="tactune-making"></span><span id="tactune-encounters"></span><span id="tactune-two-modes"></span><span id="tactune-soundscape"></span><span id="tactune-camera"></span><span id="tactune-next"></span>

With the **SUSTech 3D Printing Club**, we prepared physical **brain and animal-cell models** that learners could hold and examine. The available prints were uncoloured, so team members painted the teaching regions by hand. The physical activity offered looking, handling, naming structures and asking a facilitator as ways to explore the same subject.

<figure class="inc-model-photo" id="tactune-painting-figure">
  <img src="https://static.igem.wiki/teams/6085/wiki/10-18/hp-biology-models-printing-collage.webp" alt="A team member painting the animal-cell model beside completed painted cell and brain teaching aids" width="960" height="640" loading="lazy" decoding="async">
  <figcaption>Preparing the brain and cell teaching models. Paint provides visual distinctions; the contours provide a separate physical route for exploration.</figcaption>
</figure>

The team’s account includes encounters with sighted children, children with visual impairments and children with intellectual disabilities. Those encounters prompted a further goal: connect exploration of a region with a name and an explanation. **TacTune**, developed afterwards, is the software extension of that work.

In its digital workspace, a teacher can rotate a model, choose a structure explanation and show its text. A suitable installed local voice can read the explanation. Surface exploration offers height-based sound or colour notes, while mute preserves the textual feedback. The notes encode geometric height or a colour category. Structure names come from prepared explanations, rather than being inferred from a musical pitch.

The camera prototype adds another route: a teacher or assistant outlines regions on a camera view, then a stable fingertip indication can trigger an explanation. The camera estimates pointing; it does not measure physical contact. Occlusion and a changed viewing angle can interrupt tracking. Digital model exploration and its text/menu controls remain available without the camera.

These stages have different records. The physical models were brought to learners; the software has developer checks using synthetic inputs and a separate real-camera frame-and-release check. Target-user testing of the camera interaction and audio comfort remains ahead. The current model colours come from painted references; red, yellow and blue alone do not establish colour-blind accessibility. We need learners and teachers to help judge which regions, cues and controls are distinguishable and useful in an actual session.

### 2.4 Understanding and Expressing Choices {#before-i-sign}

**Before I Sign (签之前)** explores how someone can gather information, ask questions and express a treatment or research preference. The latest local design, dated **21 October 2026**, uses a fictional older person discussing chronic knee pain, with a separate optional memory-research invitation. It gives everyday circumstances—notes, changing information, support at home and time to ask—a place in the decision.

An earlier software prototype used timed clue-dragging and branching choices. The current design replaces time pressure with revisitable information and proposes point-and-select actions, notes, questions and ways to pause, defer or decline. A proposed two-person version gives the person and a companion different information to discuss, while keeping the person’s own wishes central. These are design changes and planned interactions: the current materials contain a narrative and interface drafts, with no confirmed complete-game release or participant session.

The access question is whether someone can find the explanation they need, return to it and say what matters to them. The game supports understanding, questions and expression of choice. It does not assess capacity to consent or replace the consent process in actual care or research.

## 3. WORLDPROOF: Build it. Break it. Fix it. {#worldproof}

<span id="worldproof-changes"></span>

**WORLDPROOF means solving real problems in the real world.** For us, a biology-related design has to meet questions about who uses it, who can challenge it and what happens when circumstances change. We organized WORLDPROOF as a Bio-hackathon where people from different fields could bring those questions into a working prototype. Its slogan is **“Build it. Break it. Fix it.”**

The team designed the challenges and activity mechanism and organized students from different academic backgrounds to take part. Building a demo was one contribution; finding a missed assumption and helping another team change its design was another. This made questioning and revision an explicit part of participation.

### 3.1 From Participation to Organizing {#worldproof-organizing}

Chenfan Liao’s route into organizing began with participating. In **April 2026**, Liao took part in a hackathon and worked on a **pet collar intended to help prevent accidental ingestion**. In a separate written exchange, Liao spoke with the creator described in our [game-design context](#game-design-conversation). Liao also introduced our team’s work and the Shokz collaboration then being planned to others. That plan did not become a completed partnership.

Liao’s observation was that relatively few of the builders encountered had biology backgrounds. On campus, Liao also met students who did not know what a hackathon involved. These observations from Liao’s own encounters led to an organizing question: could we create a biology-related event where someone could begin through a practical problem and contribute knowledge from another field?

WORLDPROOF was our response. We prepared the challenge material and the process through which participants would build, question and revise. The full event and teaching record is kept on [Education](/education#worldproof); here we focus on the entrances into the task and how participants could influence a design.

### 3.2 Builder Conversations {#builder-interviews}

<span id="listening-through-products"></span><span id="builder-memory-and-bereavement"></span><span id="builder-mothers-and-homemakers"></span><span id="builder-shouyu"></span><span id="builder-connection-to-orca"></span><span id="builder-records-and-limits"></span><span id="builder-further-interviews"></span>

A separate experience came in **late August 2026**, when Chenfan Liao attended a large hackathon as a **public judge**, tried products and had short conversations at booths. Videos were recorded and are awaiting organization. The account below draws on Liao’s recollection of those encounters, separately from Liao’s April participation and earlier written exchange.

One product explored bereavement and giving memories a place. Another addressed mothers’ and homemakers’ sense of personal worth. **手屿** offered sign-language learning through an island-based interaction. Together, these products raised design questions we could carry into our own work: what can someone choose to preserve, how can a person express priorities beyond an assigned role, and how can an unfamiliar communication method become something to practise?

These are questions drawn from product encounters, rather than advice we attribute to a builder about our later prototypes. The earlier creator’s written exchange belongs with the [starting context for our game work](#game-design-conversation).

### 3.3 Build it {#worldproof-build}

The actual challenge pack offers **eight hypothetical scenarios**: **Riverlight, FieldShift, FermentOS, ClinicNow, ProBio Home, Mossback, ClearCoast and OpenBench**. Each gives a background, a conflict, stakeholders, possible demos and reality-check examples. These are prompts for design, not eight completed field validations.

A shared scenario gives people something concrete to question before they share specialist vocabulary. In **Riverlight**, for example, contained living pollution sensors at wastewater outlets raise questions about false alarms, public explanations and responsibility. A team could make a status map, an alert interface or a sample-tracking workflow. A participant’s contribution might concern the meaning of an alert, maintenance or whose evidence is shown, alongside the biology.

**ClinicNow** places a cell-free diagnostic kit in a setting with uneven connectivity, staffing and language access. **OpenBench** asks how a community biology laboratory can welcome beginners while organizing training and review. These tasks make resource conditions and entry procedures part of the design brief. The prototype is the place to make those choices visible.

The planning document also describes an entry for teams bringing an existing project and a scenario entry for people without one. We retain these as documented design options; the available event record does not establish every planned support measure as delivered.

### 3.4 Break it {#worldproof-break}

Each team was required to act as a **red team for at least three other teams**. This gave participants a defined role in questioning someone else’s design, as well as presenting their own. The challenge was directed at an assumption or system behaviour: who could be left out, what an instruction assumed, or what might fail under the scenario’s conditions.

The planning notes ask for a challenge to identify the affected person, the failure mechanism, its possible consequence and an observable warning sign. These prompts help turn a general objection into a question the receiving team can work on. The cross-team exchange then carried that question back to the people responsible for the prototype.

The all-group change log is the record we will use to connect those exchanges with later changes. It matters that a challenge can travel from another person’s perspective into the design process, even when that person did not build the original demo.

### 3.5 Fix it {#worldproof-fix}

Each team had to optimize its project in response to **at least three suggestions from other teams** and explain the improvements in its **change log**. Liao reports that judges gave particular attention to this part of the work. The final scoring sheet has not yet been matched to the local planning draft, so we do not attach a percentage to it here.

That requirement gave feedback a place in the deliverable. A useful record connects an original difficulty, the suggestion received, the change made and what the revised demo can show. Three entries alone cannot tell that story: the reason for adopting an idea, or a documented reason for leaving it aside, helps explain whose input influenced the work.

### 3.6 Following One Project’s Evolution {#worldproof-project-evolution}

Liao recalls that the **first-prize team came mainly from mathematics and statistics, with little biology background**, and that the judging included biology expertise. This is a reason to look closely at what that particular team contributed: which assumptions they noticed, what others challenged and how they responded.

The most informative account will place the initial demo beside the cross-team challenges, the corresponding change-log entries and the final demo. The team has retained an all-group change-log table; the paired project materials are being gathered for this page. For now, this section records Liao’s account of the team’s background; the paired demo and log entries will supply the project-level account.

A complete project story should also leave room for suggestions that were not adopted, changes that could not be finished and feedback that did not help, wherever the records show them. Those details reveal how participants negotiated a real design decision.
