## A question that stayed {#0-introduction-life-has-more-than-one-bandwidth}

“What can we do with this once we have extracted it?”

At Taoyuan Subdistrict Elderly Service Center, we helped older adults extract DNA from bananas. As the activity ended, one participant asked this question. We had prepared the science and the steps, but her question made us realize how little we had said about why any of it might matter in her daily life.

We kept returning to her question. Before deciding what to teach or build, we needed to understand what people wanted to know and where they needed support. ORCA studies RNA editing for Alzheimer's disease (AD), aiming to edit APOE4 mRNA towards an APOE3-like protein product.[2] It remains research; carrying APOE ε4 is a risk factor, not a diagnosis.[1]

The [3E model below Figure 1](#hp-three-e-model) gives us a way to keep that question active. The cases below show how conversations led to a research priority, a revised resource or a decision to stop.

## Whose perspectives shaped our questions? {#whose-perspectives-shaped-our-questions}

Different questions called for different perspectives. Clinical experience could reveal the burden of appointments, procedures and repeated treatment. Researchers could challenge how we judged specificity, function and control. Older adults and service-center staff could show whether our explanations and tools fitted daily routines. Potential partners could tell us what it would take to maintain an idea beyond a student project.

These perspectives are not interchangeable. A researcher's view of treatment choices does not stand in for a patient's experience, and a service-center staff member brings a different view from a family caregiver. Our survey mainly reached younger people. We still need more direct involvement from people living with AD and their family caregivers, alongside input on manufacturing, access and long-term support.

<p class="hp-evidence-context">Literature connections added on 21 October 2026 are a retrospective reading of our records. They help us interpret existing choices and plan new questions, rather than reconstruct when those choices began.</p>

The FDA’s patient-engagement methods guidance distinguishes direct patient reports from the different information that carers, clinicians and others can provide. For our next round, we need to define whose experience each question is intended to capture and offer ways for people with AD to speak for themselves where feasible. This is a plan for improving engagement, not a patient-needs finding from the guidance. [10](https://www.fda.gov/media/139088/download)

### Everyday care and usable materials {#ms-wu-understanding-daily-life-and-care-needs-through-an-elderly-service-center}

**Ms. Wu (a pseudonym)**, an administrative receptionist at Shahe Subdistrict Elderly Service Center, helped us understand everyday needs at the center.

Some older adults find small print, spoken explanations or dense text difficult to follow. Staff also have limited time. An activity needs to fit both the people attending and the people supporting it.

**We treated readability and staff time as design requirements.** Ms. Wu pointed out small type and dense pages and suggested more pictures, less text and space for notes. The revised workbook uses shorter reading sections, illustrations, writing space and a topic guide. These changes address how people can use a resource in an ordinary day. The [Education account and version comparison](/education#2-3-a-workbook-for-older-learners-involving-users-in-revision) retain the full activity and revision process.

### What the first map left unanswered {#stakeholder-map-review}

At the **Peking–Rice Global Summit on August 30, 2026**, we presented the first version of our stakeholder map.

Presenting our first stakeholder map made us pause. Its nine stages, from the laboratory to the community, felt natural as we talked through them. Yet that smooth sequence did not explain how we had identified the stakeholders or analysed their relationships with ORCA. We could tell a story about the chain more easily than we could explain the evidence behind its structure. The long layout also made those relationships difficult to follow.

This became a starting point for revising the map. We needed to ask why each group belonged, what we had actually learned about their concerns, how they could affect or be affected by ORCA, and which relationships still needed investigation. The original slide records our thinking at the Summit; its checkmarks should not be read as a current audit of completed stakeholder consultations. A revised map will need to make the reasoning and remaining gaps clearer before we assess whether it is easier to understand.

### A relevant model we could actually work with {#advisor-model-selection}

We initially planned to use **iPSC-derived APOE4/4 astrocytes** to examine a disease-relevant cellular setting. **Prof. Zefeng Wang**, our Primary PI and a Chair Professor at SUSTech, guided the scientific rationale and phenotype selection. **Ms. Min Zhang**, a Research Technician at SUSTech and a Secondary PI, assessed whether we could culture and maintain the cells in our laboratory.

<span id="advisor-customs-bottleneck"></span>

Cell procurement forced a practical decision. After exploring domestic suppliers and BrainXell, an international supplier suggested by Wang, imported cells were delayed at customs. Wang helped investigate the procurement problem and alternatives; Zhang checked their practical suitability. **We switched to CCF-STTG1 so that the experimental work could continue**, while retaining validation in the intended astrocyte model as a future task. This change made the work feasible, but changed the biological setting in which we could interpret it.

The [18 September culture record](/notebook?track=fv&date=2026-09-18) and [DELIVER Cycle 3](/engineering#engineering-model-guidance) connect that decision to the cells used in the expression assay. Zhang's later imaging work is recorded in the functional-evaluation case above.

### What should a computational model help us decide? {#hp-model-prioritization}

Wang also helped us reconsider what our computational model should do with limited editing labels. Engineering records his advice to adopt a [pretrained LAMAR encoder](/engineering#model-lamar-cycle-01-build), then to [rank candidates for a shortlist](/engineering#model-lamar-cycle-05-design) when local efficiency regression was modest. Here the response was a change in the model's task and selection criterion, matched to a limited number of experimental follow-ups.

## What the public wanted to understand {#2-2-public-understanding-survey-addressing-what-people-want-to-know-first}

### What we asked, and who responded {#survey-purpose-and-sample}

We used an **11-question survey** to learn how people understood emerging biomedical technologies and what they would want to know before judging an unfamiliar AD treatment. It asked about background and familiarity, responses to research statements and hypothetical studies, information priorities, risk presentation and preferred formats.

The aggregate report contains **103 valid responses**: 96 respondents were aged 18–30, six were under 18 and one was aged 46–60. Five selected direct experience of cognitive disorders, including personal or family diagnosis or caregiving; 24 reported some exposure without long-term caregiving. Recruitment channels, collection dates and the criteria for a valid response are not described in the supplied report. We therefore use it as feedback from this respondent group, primarily younger people, rather than as a study of patient needs.

### Our interpretation and response {#survey-communication-response}

**We put four questions first in our explanations: what could improve, what are the risks, who is it for, and how strong is the evidence?** We gave short summaries priority over technical detail and made more room to distinguish a measured biological change from a benefit in daily life. The [Engineering note on interpreting readouts](/engineering#engineering-public-input) applies that distinction when presenting research.

The report also proposes a layered information structure: a decision summary, a visual explanation and optional detail or discussion. That structure still needs testing with older adults and caregivers. The survey's documented influence is on communication and how we explain evidence; it does not identify the origin of a particular biological module. Only aggregate results are available, so our interpretation stays at the level of the reported questions and counts.

ADI’s 2024 international report adds a different communication concern: beliefs about dementia itself, including the view that it is normal ageing. Its survey used convenience recruitment and weighted analyses; it offers wider context, not a local prevalence estimate. Our questionnaire instead asked about biomedical information and did not assess dementia stigma. Together they suggest testing both the explanation of the technology and the assumptions readers bring to it. [9](https://www.alzint.org/u/World-Alzheimer-Report-2024.pdf)

## From promising ideas to workable support {#from-promising-ideas-to-workable-support}

A useful idea also needs a setting, resources and people able to carry it through. Our Shokz discussions and Anjia Guardian work exposed different parts of that problem: agreeing on the conditions for a partnership, then defining a smaller tool we could actually build and examine.

### Shokz: finding the conditions for a partnership {#1-4-adjusting-the-implementation-path-from-a-bone-conduction-hardware-concept-to-a-software-tool}

Our conversations with **Shokz** began with an idea for our own workplace. Could bone-conduction headphones carry an AI assistant into a synthetic biology laboratory? We imagined spoken reminders to take a break or guidance through experimental steps, leaving a researcher's hands free. It was an appealing starting point, but the laboratory's firm safety requirements left little room for the use we had imagined. We set that application aside rather than treating safety as a detail to resolve after designing the product.

#### Looking beyond the laboratory

We then explored a different setting: everyday activity among older adults. Many older people remain active, and we wondered whether a familiar wearable could offer support without asking them to give up that independence. Our working analogy was a phone watch designed for an older person: a way to stay connected and find help while going about their day.

We considered adding GPS so that, if a wearer appeared uncertain about their route, the device might offer directions home and reassuring audio. We also wondered whether selected audio stimulation could have a role in supporting cognitive health. Another idea was to use head-mounted motion sensing to detect a possible fall and connect it with an inflatable protective device worn around the waist or back. **These were exploratory concepts.** We had not validated a way to infer disorientation, established a cognitive benefit from audio, or demonstrated fall protection. Whether head-mounted sensing could improve on a watch was a question for testing, not a result we had obtained.

Behind these ideas was a concern we wanted to keep: how could someone continue to move through a familiar world with confidence, while having support available when they needed it?

#### Why the collaboration did not proceed

Further discussions brought the practical requirements into focus. Shokz explained that it did not have a dedicated team for this particular application, so development would likely need support from a university laboratory. From our conversations, its priorities were connecting with innovative talent and developing university–industry relationships; demand for a new product or product promotion was less central.

We approached university laboratories in the hope of finding that technical support. The discussions did not produce a shared starting point. The laboratories we approached were looking primarily for research funding or substantial hardware support, while the opportunity we could bring centred on talent and an exploratory collaboration. We had found interest on both sides, but had not found an arrangement that met both sides' needs.

**The proposed collaboration did not proceed.** There was no jointly developed wearable or validated hardware outcome. This was disappointing, especially after imagining the everyday situations in which it might help. It also showed us something our early enthusiasm had missed: an idea needs people who can take responsibility for developing it, resources they can actually commit, and a reason for each partner to continue.

#### Cooperation proposal {#shokz-cooperation-proposal}

The Chinese proposal and its English translation document the proposed forum activities, student participation, local headphone prototype, responsibilities and evaluation targets. They preserve the planning stage of this exploration; the proposed partnership, resources and MVP targets were not completed outcomes. Other teams can adapt the structure to clarify partner expectations and conditions before committing to a collaboration.

#### What we carried forward

We learned to ask about those conditions earlier, alongside the question of whether a product sounded useful. For future partnerships, we would need to clarify each side's priorities, available support and responsibilities before building a proposal around them.

Our attention then turned towards **Anjia Guardian**, a Mini Program for everyday home support. It gave us a more manageable way to build a tool with older adults. The concern carried forward was the same: support should help people retain a say in their daily lives. The software would need its own evaluation, independently of the wearable idea.

### Anjia Guardian: making everyday support usable {#2-5-anjia-guardian-a-home-safety-mini-app-designed-for-older-adults}

The daily-life question was more immediate than a future RNA-editing treatment: could an older adult review a possible home hazard and share a concern with family without navigating a dense interface? Care-center conversations had already made readability and limited support time concrete concerns. **We brought large text, larger controls, role-based navigation and confirmations into the Mini Program.**

**The Mini Program provides the full Anjia Guardian experience.** It is the main tool for exploring home checks, safety prompts and the older-adult and family workflows. The web version is provided only as a demonstration, offering an accessible walkthrough of the interface and interaction flow.

The web demonstration uses a drawn room with two fixed hazard examples, local photo preview and sample report and SOS interactions. These examples help visitors understand the design; they do not represent the full Mini Program's feature set. The browser demo makes no AI assessment, phone call or caregiver notification.

**Older adults in Shahe tried Anjia Guardian and told us they found it useful.** We took this as encouragement to continue improving the interface. Our next evaluation needs to observe which tasks people can complete independently, the clarity of prompts and the handling of failed or uncertain reports. Any effect on safety in daily life will need separate evaluation over time.

## What changed, and what comes next {#what-changed-and-what-comes-next}

The clearest changes are different kinds of decisions. **An actual modification** was the switch to CCF-STTG1 when the intended cell model could not be obtained in time; Wang's computational guidance also changed the model's task towards candidate ranking. **A shift in priority** brought delivery burden and the interpretation of editing evidence further into our evaluation. **A stopped route** was the proposed Shokz collaboration, where interest did not become a workable match of resources and responsibilities. In everyday support, readable materials and the Anjia Guardian Mini Program gave us something concrete to revise with people.

These decisions leave a common next question: can the people affected help us judge the next version, rather than only react to a finished explanation?

### Next steps {#hp-next-steps}

We need to involve people living with AD and family caregivers more directly, test communication materials with their intended readers, and evaluate Anjia Guardian through observed tasks. In the laboratory, validation in the originally intended astrocyte model and controlled functional analysis remain ahead. The revised stakeholder map will need a clearer account of relationships and participation gaps before we assess its usefulness.

#### An information prototype for informed choices {#1-2-informed-consent-and-proxy-decision-making-designing-multiple-prototype-versions}

**A signature does not tell us what someone understood.** We want research information to help people ask questions and make their own choices.

Our planned prototype starts with a short explanation of purpose, research stage, possible benefits, risks and alternatives. Readers could then choose more detail. Large-print, audio and assisted-reading versions still need development and testing.

We also plan prompts that invite readers to explain key points in their own words. These should help us improve the explanation, without making reading ability a barrier to participation.

Older age, cognitive impairment or family accompaniment should not automatically be treated as an inability to decide. Support and proxy decisions must follow appropriate ethical and professional processes; a communication tool cannot replace them.[3]

The goal is informed choice, including a choice researchers did not expect. This prototype remains a design proposal awaiting development and reader testing.

#### Further conversations and future use {#1-3-product-development-and-commercialization-translational-questions-and-future-interviews}

Future use would depend on delivery, manufacturing, clinical evidence, affordability and ongoing support. We need to understand who would use ORCA and who would carry those responsibilities.

We completed initial Builder interviews at hackathon booths about products addressing bereavement and memory, mothers’ and homemakers’ sense of self-worth, and sign-language learning. For ORCA, these conversations raised questions about respecting personal experiences of memory, care and communication. They did not establish clinical outcomes or changes to our RNA-editing system. The interviews and planned deeper follow-ups are documented on our [Inclusivity page](/inclusivity#builder-interviews).

Workbooks and model files can support others' work. Delivery, manufacturing, access and patent options for the editing technology need further evaluation; these remain future translation questions.

### Literature and analysis methods {#hp-literature-methods}

We reviewed six sources against three questions: meaningful improvement, the work of future use and care, and barriers to understanding. We read original study methods and results or the original official report. The review supports the retrospective interpretation above; it is a scoped review, not a systematic synthesis.

<details class="hp-evidence-review-methods" markdown="1">
<summary>Search scope, study populations and limitations</summary>

Searches on **21 October 2026** used combinations of “Alzheimer meaningful outcomes patients care partners”, “China dementia caregivers treatment preferences”, “dementia China caregivers qualitative information needs”, “World Alzheimer Report 2024 attitudes methodology”, and “FDA patient-focused representative input”. We prioritized direct patient/caregiver research and relevant Chinese settings, then included an international report and a methods guide. Duplicate discussions, general AD biology reviews and abstract-only candidates without enough verifiable detail were not used for the new analysis.

- **[5] US qualitative interviews:** 60 interview units across five stage groups, with moderate-stage patient–partner dyads and severe-stage partner reports. Stage and reporter matter; these are not population preference estimates.
- **[6] Beijing qualitative research:** 10 caregiver interviews in September–December 2017 and groups with 29 primary care workers in January–February 2018, recruited through four Chaoyang community health centers. The local purposive sample does not represent all Chinese families.
- **[7] Mainland Chinese cross-sectional study:** 1,675 valid caregiver-completed questionnaires about probable-AD cases across 30 provincial-level regions, August–December 2019. Health-center recruitment and an education criterion limit coverage; associations do not establish causes.
- **[8] US online choice experiment:** 137 caregiver and 161 neurologist responses retained from 180 and 179 completers after attention/comprehension exclusions. - **[9] ADI report:** 34,619 people answered at least one stigma-related item across 166 countries/territories. Convenience, snowball and targeted recruitment were followed by weighting; item bases differ. We use qualitative context without importing a percentage into the local survey.
- **[10] FDA methods guidance:** informs how to define the population, reporter and sampling plan. It supplies no patient-needs sample.

The accessible methods did not establish fieldwork dates for [5] and [8], or exact collection dates for [9]; publication years are kept separate.
